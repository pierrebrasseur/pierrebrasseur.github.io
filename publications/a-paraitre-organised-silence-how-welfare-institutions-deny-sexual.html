Organised silence : how welfare institutions deny sexual violence against disabled children

Pierre Brasseur · International Journal of Social Welfare, à paraître

Abstract

Children with disabilities face a markedly elevated risk of sexual violence, yet this violence remains systematically under-recognised within the welfare institutions mandated to protect them. This article asks how that non-recognition is organisationally produced. Rather than reporting new primary fieldwork, it advances a conceptual framework, built from a comparative reading of the child-protection reporting regimes of France and Francophone Belgium, a synthesis of the international epidemiological and qualitative research on violence against disabled children and on professional (non-)reporting, and illustrative cases from the public record. Interpreting these materials through the sociology of denial (Cohen), organisational decoupling and street-level discretion (Meyer & Rowan; Lipsky), and testimonial injustice (Fricker), the article develops three linked propositions. First, disabled children are exposed to a compound testimonial deficit that renders their accounts of violence structurally inaudible. Second, suspicion, when it arises, is routed through an informal management corridor — minimisation, familialisation, deferral — that absorbs it below the threshold of formal reporting. Third, these routines are sustained by organisational logics — institutional loyalty, role fragmentation, and the absence of safe reporting conditions — that cultivate silence without ever commanding it. The comparison of two jurisdictions with markedly different reporting laws suggests that this organised silence is driven less by the legal framework than by the professional cultures and organisational logics of medico-social institutions.
